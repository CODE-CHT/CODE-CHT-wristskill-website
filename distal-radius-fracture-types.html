What type of distal radius fracture do you have?

By Joseph Sharaya, OTD, OTR/L, CHT · Certified Hand Therapist · Last reviewed September 2026

If you have just broken your wrist, someone has probably handed you a report with a name on it. Colles. Smith. Barton. Intra-articular. Dorsally angulated. This page explains what those words mean, in plain language, so the paperwork stops being a foreign language.

This page is education, not a diagnosis. It explains terms that a doctor has already given you. It cannot tell you which fracture you have, and it does not replace the team treating you. If something is getting worse rather than better, skip to when to be seen urgently.

What actually broke

You have two bones in your forearm. The radius runs down the thumb side, the ulna down the pinky side. "Distal" simply means the end nearest your hand. So a distal radius fracture is a break in the thumb-side forearm bone, right where it widens out to meet the wrist.

It is one of the most common fractures people have4,5, and the usual story is an ordinary one: a fall, a hand put out to catch yourself, and the whole force of the landing arriving at that widened end of the bone. Clinicians call that a fall onto an outstretched hand, and it is behind most of the patterns below.

That widened end matters, because it is not just bone. It carries the smooth cartilage surface your wrist bones glide on. Whether the break reached that surface is one of the two things every name on your report is describing.

The two questions behind every name

The named patterns look like a list of unrelated surnames, but they are really just the answers to two questions.

First: which way did the broken end move? Toward the back of your hand is called dorsal. Toward your palm is called volar or palmar. A wrist where the end has tipped backward can have a slight step in the profile, which older textbooks call a dinner fork shape.12

Second: did the fracture line reach the joint surface? If it stopped in the solid bone before the cartilage, it is extra-articular. If it ran all the way into that gliding surface, it is intra-articular.

Two questions, two answers each, and four of the names on your report fall straight out of the grid.

The four named patterns

Colles fracture

The break sits just above the wrist, stops before the joint surface, and the end tips backward toward the back of the hand.12 This is the pattern most people picture when they hear broken wrist, and it is the classic result of landing on an outstretched hand with the wrist bent back.12 It is named for Abraham Colles, who described it in 1814, eight decades before X-rays existed to confirm it.1 Worth knowing: strictly the name means an extra-articular fracture, but in everyday use it often gets applied to almost any dorsally displaced distal radius fracture.12

Smith fracture

The same location, the opposite direction. The end tips toward the palm instead of the back of the hand, which is why it is sometimes called a reverse Colles.3,13 It tends to follow a fall onto a bent-forward wrist or a direct blow to the back of the wrist.13 The classic profile is called a garden spade deformity, the counterpart to the dinner fork. Smith fractures are frequently unstable and often prove difficult to hold in a cast, so when they are displaced they usually go to surgery, most often a plate placed on the palm side.13 There is a second reason surgeons care about correcting the volar shift: left displaced, it narrows the entrance to the carpal tunnel and can crowd the nerve that runs through it.13

Dorsal Barton fracture

Here the fracture line runs into the joint surface and shears off the back rim of the radius. The important part is what happens next: the wrist bones follow that rim backward, so the joint itself is out of position, not just the bone.14 That combination is why this pattern is generally treated as unstable and usually fixed surgically.14

Volar Barton fracture

The mirror image. The palm-side rim shears off and the wrist bones slide forward with it. Both Barton patterns are named for John Rhea Barton, who described the injury in 18382, and both are usually held with a plate placed against the broken rim to buttress it.14 Between them they account for only a small share of distal radius fractures, somewhere between one and four in a hundred.14

Four names, not all of them. These four are organised by direction and joint involvement, but other named patterns sit outside that grid — a chauffeur fracture and a die-punch fracture are both described below. Plenty of real fractures fit none of them cleanly, and many reports describe the pattern in measurements instead of a surname. That is normal and says nothing unusual about your wrist.

Other words on your report

A radiology report is written for the surgeon, not for you. These are the terms that most often send people searching.

  • Displaced means the broken pieces have moved out of line with each other. Nondisplaced or undisplaced means they are still lined up.
  • Comminuted means the bone broke into more than two pieces.
  • Angulation, usually with a number of degrees, describes how far the end has tipped and in which direction.
  • Intra-articular means the fracture line reached the cartilage surface of the joint. Extra-articular means it did not.
  • Radial shortening means the broken end settled and the bone now sits slightly shorter than it was.
  • Ulnar styloid fracture refers to the small bony bump on the pinky side. It very commonly breaks alongside the radius and often needs no separate treatment. What decides whether it matters is usually the stability of the joint between the two forearm bones, not the broken tip itself9.
  • Chauffeur fracture, also called a Hutchinson fracture, is a break of the radial styloid, the bony point on the thumb side. It runs into the joint surface, and because strong ligaments anchor at that point surgeons also assess the scapholunate ligament, which is injured alongside it in a large share of these fractures.8 The name is a leftover from hand-cranked car engines kicking back.
  • Die-punch fracture describes a piece of the joint surface driven downward into the bone, most often the part the lunate sits on. It is caused by the lunate being driven down into the radius under load, and because it leaves a depression in the cartilage surface it gets close attention10. The name comes from the tool-and-die trade rather than from the bone.10
  • Percutaneous pinning means wires passed through the skin to hold the pieces, usually removed later in clinic. An external fixator holds the bone from a frame outside the arm.
  • Closed reduction means the pieces were pushed back into position without opening the skin. Open reduction and internal fixation, shortened to ORIF, means surgery to position the pieces and hold them, often with a plate and screws on the palm side.

Does the name decide the treatment?

Not on its own. The name describes a shape. What actually drives the decision is how far the pieces moved, whether the joint surface is involved and by how much, whether the fracture will hold its position once it is set, the quality of the bone, your age, and what your hand needs to do afterward. A carpenter and a retired librarian with identical X-rays can reasonably be offered different plans.

Broadly, undisplaced fractures that will stay put are often managed in a cast or splint. Fractures that have moved may be pushed back into place and then held. Fractures that will not hold, or that leave a step in the joint surface, are the ones most likely to be offered surgery.6 Which of those describes you is a conversation with your surgeon, and it is a fair thing to ask directly.

What usually comes next

Whatever the pattern, a wrist that has been held still while bone heals comes out stiff. Swelling, a weak grip and a hand that feels like it belongs to someone else are all ordinary at that point, and they surprise people who assumed the cast coming off was the finish line. It is closer to the halfway mark.

Most teams encourage keeping the fingers, elbow and shoulder moving from early on, precisely so that the stiffness is confined to the part that had to be still. After immobilisation, hand therapy usually focuses on getting motion back before strength, and current rehabilitation guidance puts a home exercise programme taught by a clinician at the centre of that.7 Follow the instructions from your own team over anything you read here, including this page.

Recovery is measured in months rather than weeks, and the tail of it is longer than most people expect.5,7 That is not a sign something has gone wrong.

When to be seen urgently

Most of a fracture recovery is uneventful. These are the signs that should not wait for the next scheduled appointment.

  • Numbness or pins and needles that is getting worse, especially across the thumb, index, middle and half the ring finger. Pressure on the nerve inside the carpal tunnel complicates a small but real share of these fractures and is treated urgently11
  • Pain that is escalating rather than settling, particularly if straightening the fingers makes it much worse
  • Fingers that turn pale, blue or cold, or swelling that keeps increasing
  • A cast or splint that feels dangerously tight, or burning and rubbing underneath it
  • Fever, spreading redness, or fluid coming from a surgical wound

If you cannot reach your surgeon or therapist quickly and any of the above is happening, urgent care or an emergency department is the right call. Pressure problems and nerve problems after a wrist fracture are treatable, and they are treated best early.

Common questions

What is a Colles fracture?

A Colles fracture is a break at the end of the radius, the forearm bone on the thumb side, in which the broken end tips backward toward the back of the hand. It is the most familiar pattern and usually follows a fall onto an outstretched hand. Strictly the name means the fracture line stopped before the wrist joint surface, though in everyday use it gets applied more loosely.

What is the difference between a Colles fracture and a Smith fracture?

The difference is direction. In a Colles fracture the broken end tips backward, toward the back of the hand. In a Smith fracture it tips the other way, toward the palm, which is why it is sometimes called a reverse Colles. A Smith fracture more often follows a fall onto a bent-forward wrist or a blow to the back of the wrist.

Is a Colles fracture worse than a Smith fracture?

Neither name is a severity rating, and how far the pieces moved and whether the joint surface is involved matter far more than which surname is on the report. That said, Smith fractures are more often unstable and harder to hold in a cast, so as a group they come to surgery more often.

What does intra-articular mean on a wrist X-ray report?

Intra-articular means the fracture line reached the smooth cartilage surface where the radius meets the wrist bones. Extra-articular means it stopped short of that surface. Surgeons watch this closely, because a break that crosses the joint surface can leave a step or a gap in the cartilage, and that can change how the fracture is treated.

What is a Barton fracture?

A Barton fracture is a break through the rim of the radius at the wrist joint in which the broken rim piece slides out of place and the wrist bones follow it. It is called dorsal when the back rim goes and volar or reverse when the palm-side rim goes. Because the joint surface and the position of the wrist are both involved, it is usually treated as unstable and held with a plate against the broken rim.

What is a chauffeur's fracture?

A chauffeur's fracture, also called a Hutchinson fracture, is a break of the radial styloid, the bony point on the thumb side of the wrist. The fracture line runs into the joint surface. Strong ligaments anchor at that point, so surgeons also assess the scapholunate ligament, which is injured alongside it in a large share of these fractures. The name dates from hand-cranked car engines kicking back against the wrist.

My report mentions a broken ulnar styloid. What does that mean?

The ulnar styloid is the small bony bump on the pinky side of the wrist, at the end of the other forearm bone. It is broken alongside a distal radius fracture very often. On its own it frequently needs no separate treatment, and what usually decides whether it matters is the stability of the joint between the two forearm bones rather than the broken tip itself.

My report has no surname on it at all. Is that bad?

No. Many reports describe the fracture in measurements and location rather than reaching for an eponym, and that carries more information, not less. Names like Colles and Smith are shorthand, and shorthand loses detail.

Does the type of distal radius fracture change how long recovery takes?

The name describes the pattern, not the timeline. Recovery is shaped by how far the pieces moved, whether the joint surface is involved, how stable the fracture is, bone quality, age and what the hand has to do afterward. Two people with the same fracture name can have very different courses, so the timeline is a question for the team treating you.

Is stiffness normal after a wrist fracture?

Yes. A wrist held still while a fracture heals is almost always stiff when it comes out, and swelling and a weak grip are common at that stage too. Regaining motion is the usual focus of hand therapy afterward. Stiffness that is not improving, or pain and swelling that are getting worse rather than better, is worth reporting to your team.

Will my wrist go back to exactly how it was?

Many people regain function they are happy with, and some are left with a degree of stiffness, aching in cold weather or reduced end-range motion. Where you land depends on the injury, the treatment and the rehabilitation. It is worth asking your own team what is realistic for your fracture rather than reasoning from averages.

Should I be doing exercises now?

Only the ones your team has given you. Exercise that is right at one stage of healing can be wrong at another, and a page on the internet cannot know which stage you are at.

Getting the motion back

The stiffness after a wrist fracture is the part hand therapists spend the most time on. If you have not been referred to one and your wrist is not moving the way you expect, it is reasonable to ask your doctor whether hand therapy would help.
