How Often Should Kids Shower? An Age-by-Age Guide

By Joseph Sharaya, OTD, OTR/L, CHT · Occupational therapist · September 10, 2026

Less often than most parents assume, until puberty, when it changes to daily. Below is the age-by-age answer with the sources it comes from, the separate and often-missed question of how often to wash hair, what actually counts as a wash, and how to run different schedules for different children without losing track.

The short answer

AgeBathing or showeringSource
First yearAround 3 baths a week is enough. More can dry the skin.AAP
Toddler to age 5No fixed rule. Bathe when dirty, and adjust to the child.—
Ages 6 to 11At least once or twice a week when not dirty or sweaty. Daily is also fine.AAD
Puberty onwardShower or bathe daily. Wash the face twice a day.AAD
Any ageAdd a wash for dirt, sweat, body odour, sport, and after a pool, lake or ocean.AAD
Skin conditionsFollow the frequency a dermatologist gives you, which may differ from all of the above.AAD

There is no single correct number, and that is the most useful thing on this page. Frequency depends on age, activity, skin condition, climate, and the individual child. Picking your household's number and holding it is worth more than finding a perfect one.

Age by age, and why the number changes

Babies: about three baths a week

The American Academy of Pediatrics advises that three baths per week during a baby's first year may be enough, and notes that bathing more often can dry out a baby's skin. Newborns rarely sweat or get soiled enough to need daily bathing, provided the nappy area is cleaned thoroughly at each change.

Toddlers and preschoolers: when they need it

There is no authoritative fixed frequency for this range, and any source that gives you one is inventing precision. Toddlers get visibly dirty, which makes the decision easy most days. Bath time also often serves purposes beyond hygiene at this age, from play to a bedtime cue, and that is a legitimate reason to bathe more often than strictly necessary.

Ages 6 to 11: once or twice a week is genuinely enough

This is where parental assumption and dermatology diverge most. The American Academy of Dermatology's position is that children aged 6 to 11 need a bath at least once or twice a week when they are not visibly dirty or sweaty, and that a daily bath is also fine if that is what your household prefers.

Both halves of that matter. Twice a week is not neglect, and daily is not excessive. If you have been fighting nightly about a shower a dermatologist would not require, that is worth knowing before the next round.

Puberty onward: daily

The recommendation changes because the body does. The AAD advises showering or bathing daily once puberty starts, washing the face twice a day, and bathing after sweating or sport.

The driver is a new kind of sweat gland. As the American Academy of Pediatrics explains, pre-teens and teens develop increased apocrine gland sweating, which puts oil into the sweat that skin bacteria break down into odour. The AAP notes this often starts as early as age 8 or 9, which is earlier than many parents expect, and suggests showering daily or every other day once body odour is noticeable.

Worth saying plainly to a child at this stage: the rule changed because your body changed, not because you have started smelling and are in trouble.

How often should kids wash their hair?

This is a separate schedule from bathing, and it is the question most guides skip. Hair frequency depends heavily on hair type, which bathing frequency does not.

The AAD's guidance on washing children's hair:

SituationShampoo
Ages 8 to 11Once or twice a week
Age 12 or puberty, with oily or straight hair, and activeEvery other day, or daily
Dry or curly hair, any ageEvery 7 to 10 days

The dry or curly exception is the important one, and it holds regardless of age. Washing curly or dry hair as often as straight hair strips it. After swimming or heavy sweating, the AAD suggests rinsing and conditioning rather than shampooing for these hair types.

The practical consequence: in a household with two children of different hair types, they are on two different hair schedules, and neither matches their shower schedule. That is three separate cadences in one bathroom, which is exactly why this gets tracked badly.

What counts as a wash?

More than parents tend to assume, and this matters on the hard days.

  • A bath and a shower are interchangeable for hygiene purposes. Use whichever your child tolerates.
  • A wash at the sink covers most of it. Face, hands, feet, underarms and groin is the set that matters most for odour and skin health. On a day when a full bath is not happening, this is a legitimate substitute rather than a failure.
  • A rinse after swimming counts as the post-pool wash the AAD recommends, and for dry or curly hair a rinse and condition is the recommended handling.
  • Hair does not have to be washed every time the body is. Separating the two is often what makes a daily shower tolerable for a child who hates having their hair washed.

Can kids bathe too often?

Yes, mainly through its effect on skin. Frequent and long hot washing strips the skin barrier and worsens dryness and eczema. The AAD's dry-skin guidance is to limit baths and showers to five to ten minutes using warm rather than hot water, to pat rather than rub the skin dry, and to moisturise while the skin is still damp.

If your child has eczema or another skin condition, the frequency their dermatologist gives you overrides the general guidance on this page, in either direction. Some skin conditions call for more bathing, not less.

Running different schedules for different kids

Once you have the numbers, a second and much more boring problem appears: keeping track. A household with a nine-year-old on twice a week, a thirteen-year-old on daily, and two different hair schedules is running four cadences, none of which leaves any evidence.

That is why the recall question is so unreliable. Nobody can say confidently when a particular child last washed their hair, and the schedule ends up living in one parent's head, which is the arrangement that turns into nagging.

Any shared record fixes it. A grid on the fridge works. A bathroom whiteboard works. Shower Tally is the version we build: each person gets a card with their own schedule, hair washes track separately from showers, and the board is shared across the household's phones so it is not one person's job to remember. For a single child in one home, the fridge grid is often the better answer and costs nothing.

What if they refuse?

Frequency is the easy half. If the schedule is settled and the shower still is not happening, the problem is somewhere else, and it is usually one of these:

  • A nightly argument. Most shower fights are negotiations, and several turn out to be disputes about a fact neither party knows. See getting kids to shower without a fight.
  • The shower itself is unpleasant. Water on the face, noise, temperature, the cold exit. For autistic children in particular this is often the whole problem. See helping an autistic child shower.
  • Starting is the hard part. Common with ADHD, and it responds to structure rather than reminders. See why showering feels impossible with ADHD.
  • You are the only one tracking it. See the mental load is real.
A note: this is general information, not medical advice. Talk to your child's paediatrician or a dermatologist about eczema, persistent skin problems, unusually early body odour, or any skin condition, since the right frequency in those cases can differ from everything above.

FAQ

How often should kids shower?

It depends on age. The American Academy of Dermatology says children aged 6 to 11 need a bath at least once or twice a week when they are not dirty or sweaty, and that daily is also fine. Once puberty starts, it recommends daily. Add a wash for dirt, sweat, sport, and swimming.

Should kids shower every day?

Before puberty, they do not need to, though it does no harm if baths are kept short and warm rather than hot. After puberty, daily is the recommendation, because apocrine sweat glands become active and produce the oils that skin bacteria turn into body odour.

How often should a 7 year old bathe?

At least once or twice a week if they are not visibly dirty or sweaty, per the American Academy of Dermatology, with more when they get muddy, sweaty, or have been swimming. Daily is also fine.

How often should a teenager shower?

Daily, plus after sweating or sport, and washing the face twice a day. The AAP notes body odour can begin as early as age 8 or 9, so the change often comes before parents expect it.

How often should kids wash their hair?

Ages 8 to 11, once or twice a week. From age 12 or puberty, every other day or daily if the hair is oily or straight and the child is active. Dry or curly hair at any age, every 7 to 10 days. Hair is a separate schedule from bathing.

Does a sink wash count instead of a bath?

For most purposes yes. Washing face, hands, feet, underarms and groin covers what matters most for odour and skin health, and it is a reasonable substitute on a day when a full bath is not going to happen.

Can bathing too often be bad for kids?

Frequent long hot washing dries the skin and can worsen eczema. The AAD recommends keeping baths and showers to five to ten minutes in warm water, patting the skin dry, and moisturising while it is still damp.

How do I keep track of different schedules for different children?

Use one shared record rather than several people's memories, because a household can easily be running four cadences at once between bathing and hair. A grid on the fridge, a bathroom whiteboard, or a shared app all work; the tool matters much less than everyone reading from the same one.

What if my child refuses regardless of the schedule?

Frequency is the easy half. Work out whether the obstacle is the nightly negotiation, the sensory experience of the shower itself, difficulty getting started, or a schedule nobody can verify. Each has a different fix.
