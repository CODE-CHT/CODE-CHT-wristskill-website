My Teen Won't Shower: What's Behind It and What Actually Helps

By Joseph Sharaya, OTD, OTR/L, CHT · Occupational therapist · September 14, 2026

A teenager who won't shower is not necessarily lazy, and treating it as laziness tends to backfire. Hygiene can slip in the teen years for ordinary reasons: the body needs more washing than it used to, nobody spelled out the details, mornings are short, and being told what to do about your body is hard to hear at fourteen. Sometimes the reason is ADHD, sensory discomfort, or depression. Here is how to tell which, what helps, and when to call the pediatrician.

If your child is younger and the problem is a nightly standoff, start with getting kids to shower without a fight. This article is about what changes in the teen years.

The short answer

  • The need really did change. Once puberty starts, dermatologists recommend a daily shower or bath, and one after sports or heavy sweating.
  • Find the reason first. Not knowing, no time, trouble starting, disliking the shower itself, and feeling low need different fixes.
  • Talk privately, without shaming. Shaming does not work in the long run and is linked to worse outcomes in teens.
  • Let your teen own the routine, with an agreed minimum and consequences tied to the routine, not their character.
  • Call the pediatrician if poor hygiene comes with signs of depression lasting more than two weeks. If your teen talks about suicide, call or text 988.

Why teens stop showering

Their body changed faster than their routine

For a nine-year-old who is not dirty or sweaty, a bath once or twice a week can be enough, according to the American Academy of Dermatology's guidance for ages 6 to 11. Once puberty starts, that becomes a daily shower or bath, washing the face twice a day, and bathing after swimming, sports, or heavy sweating.

The American Academy of Pediatrics explains that pre-teens and teens develop increased apocrine gland sweating, releasing oil that skin bacteria turn into odor, often from as early as age 8 or 9. The AAP's rule is slightly looser than the AAD's: daily or every other day once body odor is noticeable, noting that teens who are very active or in sports may especially need a daily shower. A routine that fit a few years ago may no longer be enough, and your teen may not have noticed. The age-by-age detail is in our guide to how often kids should shower.

Nobody spelled out the details

Rinsing off is not the whole job, so be specific rather than assuming. The AAP's advice:

  • Soap and water, especially on armpits, feet, and groin. Scalps sweat too, so shampoo is part of it.
  • Deodorant, with or without antiperspirant. Some scented body sprays contain no true deodorant or antiperspirant ingredients; they cover up smells but do not always prevent odor.
  • Fresh clothes after sweating, and socks worn with shoes and changed often.

The AAP notes that the body changes of early adolescence can bring anxiety, especially when a young person does not know what is normal, so say plainly that it happens to everyone.

Privacy and control matter more now

According to the AAP, pre-teens feel an increased need for privacy, are often self-conscious about their appearance, and may react strongly when parents reinforce limits. Many middle adolescents argue more with their parents as they push for independence. A comment about hygiene touches all of it at once: their body, how others see them, and who gets to decide. Some of the pushback may be about being told, not about the shower.

Mornings are short, and planning is still developing

The National Institute of Mental Health explains that in adolescence melatonin stays high later at night and drops later in the morning, and many teens do not get enough sleep. A morning shower competes with sleep they are already short on. NIMH also notes that the prefrontal cortex, which handles skills like planning and prioritizing, is one of the last parts of the brain to mature. A teen can agree that a shower is needed and still not get from agreeing to doing without some structure.

Starting is hard, or the shower feels bad

ADHD. NIMH's signs of inattention include forgetting daily activities such as chores and difficulty organizing tasks and managing time, and inattention typically continues into adolescence. A daily shower is that kind of task. Our article on why showering feels impossible with ADHD is written for adults, but its account of how hard it is to start a boring, multi-step task may ring true for a teen too.

Sensory discomfort. For some teens the obstacle is the shower itself: water on the face, the noise, the cold air on the way out. This is not limited to autism. In a representative sample of 925 children aged 7 to 11, 16% of parents reported that at least four touch or sound sensations bothered their child, and a systematic review found sensory processing problems more common in children with ADHD than in typically developing children, while calling the area not well studied. Both concern children rather than teens. Our guide to helping an autistic child shower covers practical changes to the shower itself.

Sometimes it is a sign of something heavier

Poor hygiene alone is not a diagnosis, but it can be part of a larger change. The AAP lists lacking interest in personal grooming or appearance among the signs of depression in teens, alongside less energy or motivation for even simple tasks, more time alone, and changes in sleep or eating. Any one of these can occur in a teen who is not depressed. When they show up together, nearly every day, the AAP calls them red flags.

Adolescence is also when many mental illnesses emerge, NIMH notes. Far less often, poor hygiene is an early sign of psychosis: NIMH lists a decline in self-care or personal hygiene among the behavioral warning signs for psychosis, along with social withdrawal, trouble thinking clearly, suspiciousness, and a sudden drop in grades. Psychosis is uncommon, with an estimated 15 to 100 people out of 100,000 developing it each year, and it often begins in the late teens to mid-20s. Early treatment often means better recovery.

How to get your teenager to shower

An honest caveat first: we could not find any trials testing ways to get typically developing teenagers to shower. What follows draws on American Academy of Pediatrics guidance on adolescence and discipline, and on common practice. Treat it as well-reasoned rather than proven.

1. Have one private conversation, without shame

The AAP suggests talking with your teen about caring for their body, and how you say it matters. Its policy statement on discipline concludes that yelling at or shaming children is minimally effective in the short term and not effective in the long term. For teens the warning is sharper. In a study of 976 families, harsh verbal discipline at age 13, including shouting, swearing, or calling a child dumb or lazy, predicted increases in conduct problems and depressive symptoms by age 14, and parental warmth did not buffer it. The study was observational, and the link ran both ways: teen misbehavior also predicted harsher discipline. Still, "you're lazy and you smell" is the kind of remark it describes.

  • Choose a private moment, away from siblings and friends, and not mid-argument.
  • Make it about the body, not the person. "Your body has changed, so the old routine isn't enough anymore. That happens to everyone."
  • Give the facts, then stop. What to wash, how often, and why.
  • Ask what gets in the way. "No time," "the bathroom is freezing," and "I forget" need different fixes.

2. Remove the friction

  • Let them choose their own products and keep them stocked. For sensitive skin, the AAP suggests fragrance-free or aluminum-free deodorants.
  • Protect their privacy with a knock-first rule and a door that closes.
  • Try evenings if mornings are the problem, so the shower is not competing with sleep.
  • Separate hair from body. Even at the start of puberty, the AAD's guidance is to shampoo dry or curly hair, including braids and weaves, every 7 to 10 days. A shower without a hair wash is still a shower.
  • Fix the shower if the shower is the problem: a handheld showerhead, a warmer bathroom, a towel within reach.

3. Let them design the routine

The AAP advises parents of adolescents to set clear limits with high but reasonable expectations while gradually expanding independence as teens take on responsibility, a balance it associates with lower rates of depression and drug use. For showering, agree on a minimum together and hand over the rest.

  • The minimum: for example, daily or every other day, and always after practice.
  • Theirs to decide: morning or night, how long, which products, what music.
  • Reminders they set on their own phone, not reminders you deliver out loud.

The tools in our guide for younger kids, like deciding the schedule in advance and saying it once, can still help. The difference is that your teen helps write the schedule.

4. Tie consequences to the routine, not to who they are

If the agreed minimum keeps getting missed, a consequence is reasonable. AAP guidance for parents of teens says natural and logical consequences remain useful, and suggests letting your teen propose the consequence, on the reasoning that they are more likely to follow one that was their idea. That is experienced advice rather than a tested finding. Keep the consequence tied to the routine. "Shower first, then you can go out" is a rule. "Nobody wants to be around you" is a verdict on the person, and the kind of remark the research above links to worse outcomes.

When to talk to a pediatrician or mental health professional

Your teen's pediatrician is a good first call, and many pediatricians screen for depression every year from ages 12 through 21. If you are wondering about mood, ask directly. The AAP notes that adolescents often do not bring up their feelings but may respond honestly when asked. Make an appointment if poor hygiene comes with:

  • Signs of depression lasting more than two weeks, such as a low or irritable mood, lost interest in things they used to enjoy, pulling away from friends, low energy, or changes in sleep or appetite.
  • Withdrawal plus changes in thinking, such as confused speech, unusual ideas, suspiciousness or paranoid ideas, or trouble telling reality from fantasy. Ask for a prompt evaluation.
  • Forgetfulness and disorganization everywhere, not just in the bathroom. NIMH notes that stress, sleep disorders, anxiety, and depression can cause symptoms similar to ADHD, so this needs a proper evaluation.
  • Constant or excessive sweating, or sweating that affects their mental health or social life, which the AAP says is worth raising with the doctor.
  • Real distress in the shower, or trouble with self-care beyond reluctance. Ask about occupational therapy, which helps children and teens master basic life skills such as bathing and works on sensory processing problems.

If your teen talks about suicide or self-harm, do not wait for an appointment. Call or text the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline at 988, and in an emergency, go to an emergency room or call 911. NIMH's Teen Depression: More Than Just Moodiness is written for teens, if you want something to share with yours.

A note: this article is general information, not medical or mental health advice, and Shower Tally is not a treatment for anything. If you are worried about your teen, their pediatrician is the right place to start.

FAQ

Is it normal for a teenager to refuse to shower?

It can have ordinary explanations: the body needs more washing after puberty, teens want more privacy and control, and mornings are short. It deserves a closer look when it comes with low mood, withdrawal, or changes in sleep or appetite lasting more than two weeks.

How often should a teenager shower?

Once puberty starts, the American Academy of Dermatology recommends a daily shower or bath, washing the face twice a day, and bathing after sports or heavy sweating. The American Academy of Pediatrics says daily or every other day once body odor is noticeable.

How do I get my teenager to shower without a fight?

Have one private, calm conversation about what changed in their body, without shaming. Agree on a minimum together, such as daily or every other day and always after sports, and let your teen decide the rest, including when, which products, and whether to set reminders.

Can not showering be a sign of depression in teens?

It can be one sign among several. The American Academy of Pediatrics lists lacking interest in personal grooming or appearance among the signs of depression in teens, along with low energy, more time alone, and changes in sleep or eating. If signs last more than two weeks, talk with the pediatrician.

Should I take away privileges if my teen won't shower?

A consequence is reasonable if an agreed routine keeps getting missed, and AAP guidance says natural and logical consequences remain useful with teens. Keep the consequence tied to the routine, and avoid insults and shaming, which are linked to more behavior problems and depressive symptoms in teens.

When should I worry about my teen's hygiene?

Talk to a pediatrician if poor hygiene comes with signs of depression lasting more than two weeks, with withdrawal and confused or unusual thinking, or with excessive sweating. If your teen talks about suicide or self-harm, call or text 988 right away, or call 911 in an emergency.
