When Can Kids Shower Alone? An Occupational Therapist's Readiness Checklist

By Joseph Sharaya, OTD, OTR/L, CHT · Occupational therapist · September 14, 2026

There is no official age at which a child is ready to shower alone. The closest thing is a minimum, not a milestone: the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends that children not bathe alone until they are at least 6. Past that, readiness depends on skills and safety rather than a birthday. Below are the safety setup, a readiness checklist, and a staged handover.

Why there is no single right age

Showering alone is several skills stacked together: the motor skills to get in, wash, and get out; the sequencing to finish every step in order; the safety judgment to handle hot water or a slip; and, eventually, the wish for privacy. One lagging piece is enough to mean not yet. That breakdown is occupational therapy practice guidance, not a research finding.

The pieces also arrive at different times. Lurie Children's Hospital of Chicago's occupational therapy self-care milestone guide lists 3- to 4-year-olds as able to bathe themselves apart from washing their own hair, yet still lists supervision for bathing at 5 to 6, for safety and for how well the job gets done. The ability to wash comes well before an adult can safely step away.

Nemours KidsHealth makes the same point: children mature at different rates, and some may be ready to be left alone in the tub at 6 or 7, while others need a parent nearby for longer.

What the AAP's age 6 means

The minimum comes from the AAP's drowning prevention guidance, where the bathtub is the concern. It is the age below which a child should not bathe alone, not proof that every 6-year-old is ready. As practice guidance, apply it to showers too, because scalds and falls do not need a full tub.

The safety setup comes first

Drowning: why young children need an adult there

Drowning is the leading cause of death for children aged 1 to 4, according to the CDC. The AAP warns that a young child can drown in an inch or two of water, quickly and quietly, and that most child drownings inside the home happen in bathtubs, usually during a lapse in adult supervision. It advises staying within arm's length of young children at bath time, and not letting a young sibling supervise a child under 6. The CDC's version: have a responsible adult supervise closely and constantly whenever children are in or near water, bathtubs included.

Scalds: turn the water heater down

The AAP recommends setting your water heater so the hottest water at the faucet is no more than 120°F (48.9°C), and the Consumer Product Safety Commission urges the same. According to the CPSC, most tap-water scald injuries and deaths happen to older adults and children under 5. Its figures for adult skin show how fast the danger climbs: third-degree burns after two seconds in 150°F water, burns after six seconds at 140°F or thirty seconds at 130°F, and a possible third-degree burn after five minutes even at 120°F. Nemours KidsHealth adds that children under 5 have thinner skin that burns more easily.

The CPSC suggests checking with a candy or meat thermometer under the faucet, and asking the building manager if you rent. Testing water by hand is a good habit to teach, but it does not replace a safe maximum.

Falls: fix the floor, not just the supervision

A national study of US emergency department data from 1990 to 2007 estimated an average of 43,600 bathtub- and shower-related injuries a year in children, 81% from slips, trips or falls, and more than half in children 4 or younger. Watching may not be enough. In a smaller study of 204 children at one pediatric emergency department, an adult was already supervising in about 85% of the injuries to children under 5, and the authors argued that passive fixes, such as a slip-resistant tub surface, prevent these injuries more effectively.

The AAP's bathroom safety tips include no-slip strips on the bottom of the tub, and Nemours KidsHealth suggests them or a bath mat that grips the tub, plus wiping up spills promptly. The AAP also advises making sure any bathroom door lock can be opened from the outside.

The readiness checklist

This checklist is practice guidance, not a validated test; we could not find research on when children are ready to shower alone. Look for each item across several ordinary showers, not one good night.

AreaYour child reliably...
WaterTurns the water on, sets it to warm, tests it by hand before stepping in, and turns it off. The AAP suggests teaching children to start the cold before the hot; with a single lever, teach which way is cooler.
Too hotSteps out of the spray, turns the water toward cold or off, and calls you.
In and outSteps in and out steadily without grabbing the curtain, and stays balanced with eyes closed while rinsing hair.
WashingWashes every area in order, including underarms, groin, bottom and feet, and rinses off all the soap.
HairShampoos and rinses out all the suds. Lurie's guide lists hair as the exception when 3- to 4-year-olds start bathing themselves.
Drying and dressingDries off on the mat, wipes up puddles, and gets dressed.
Asking for helpActually calls you when the water will not adjust, soap in the eyes will not rinse out, they slip or get hurt, or they feel unwell.
House rulesLeaves the door unlocked, does not fill the tub or play with the faucets, and follows the routine without reminders.
Wanting toAsks to do it alone. Willingness is part of readiness.

One "not yet" is enough to keep an adult close, and it shows you which step to practice next. For how often hair needs washing at each age, see how often kids should shower and wash their hair.

A gradual handover, from full help to on their own

These stages are practice guidance. Stages 1 to 3 keep an adult within arm's reach, which is where a child under 6 should be. Move on after a run of uneventful showers, and expect to step back after an illness, a move, or a new bathroom.

  1. Hands-on. You set the water and do most of the washing. Your child does what they can and names each step.
  2. Side by side. Your child washes while you coach and finish the hair rinse. You still set the water.
  3. Hands off, within reach. Your child runs the routine and practices setting and testing the water while you watch.
  4. At the door. You step outside with the door ajar, stay within earshot, and check in now and then.
  5. Nearby. Door closed but unlocked, you elsewhere at home, with a quick check at the end on hair rinsing.
  6. On their own. Occasional spot checks, framed as checking the routine rather than the child.

Privacy and body autonomy as children grow

The AAP's body-safety advice is a good frame here. It recommends teaching children the correct names for body parts, which parts are private, and that their body is theirs to control. It counts a caregiver's help with bathing as an OK touch and names bath time as a good moment to talk about personal safety. Most young children cannot fully grasp modesty yet, it notes, but you can lay groundwork, such as teaching younger siblings to give older ones privacy when asked. Nemours KidsHealth adds that older kids and teens should be given privacy in the bathroom.

Day to day, as practice guidance:

  • Ask before you help. "Want to rinse, or should I?" keeps your child in charge of their body, even at the hands-on stages.
  • Hand over private areas first. Let your child wash their own genitals and bottom as soon as they can, with you coaching in words.
  • Knock and say what you are doing before you come in.
  • Treat a request for privacy as a readiness signal, not defiance. If the skills are not there yet, offer a middle step: curtain closed, you just outside, door unlocked.

Children who need more support

Seizures

According to the CDC, people with seizure disorders are at higher risk of drowning, the bathtub is the most common place it happens for them, and one-on-one supervision around water is advised. The AAP's seizure safety tips say children with seizures should never bathe without close supervision and should shower rather than bathe; if a bath does happen, keep the water low and ideally stay with them. They also advise an unlocked bathroom door, shower doors of safety glass, plastic, or a curtain, and, for children whose seizures are not controlled, hot water no hotter than 110°F.

Autism

The CDC lists autism among the conditions that can increase drowning risk and advises extra precaution around water. The AAP notes that autistic children may be drawn to water without always recognizing the danger. If bathing itself is the struggle, see how to help an autistic child shower.

Physical and motor difficulties

Children's Health in Dallas lists teaching self-care, bathing included, and the use of bathing equipment among what its pediatric occupational therapists do, and suggests asking your child's doctor about a referral when self-care skills are a struggle. As practice guidance, common options include a shower chair or bench, a handheld showerhead for washing seated, grab bars, and long-handled sponges. For some children, independence means doing most of the routine once an adult sets it up, or directing someone else's help. That still counts.

A note: this is general information, not medical advice. Ask your child's pediatrician or neurologist what level of supervision fits if your child has seizures, developmental concerns, or any condition affecting balance, awareness, or the ability to feel hot water.

FAQ

What age can a child shower alone?

There is no official age at which a child is ready. The American Academy of Pediatrics sets a minimum instead: no bathing alone before age 6. After that, it depends on skills and safety. Some children are ready at 6 or 7, and others need a parent nearby for longer.

Can a 5-year-old shower alone?

Not without an adult close by. The AAP recommends against bathing alone before age 6 and advises staying within arm's length of young children at bath time. A 5-year-old can still practice most of the routine with you right there.

How do I teach my child to shower independently?

Hand it over in stages. Start with the steps your child can already do, keep an adult in charge of the water until your child can set and test it reliably, and move yourself gradually from beside them to the doorway to elsewhere in the home.

Is a shower safer than a bath for kids?

A shower with the drain open has no standing water, and the AAP and CDC suggest showers rather than baths for children with seizure disorders. But slips, trips and falls cause most emergency department visits for bath and shower injuries in children, and hot water can scald in either.

What temperature should the water heater be set to?

Set it so the hottest water at the faucet is no more than 120°F (48.9°C), as the AAP recommends. Check it with a thermometer under the faucet. For children with uncontrolled seizures, the AAP's seizure guidance suggests 110°F or less. Testing water by hand is a good habit, not a safeguard.

When should I give my child privacy in the bathroom?

Gradually, and as early as safety allows. The AAP encourages teaching children that their body is theirs to control, and Nemours KidsHealth says older kids and teens should be given privacy in the bathroom. A closed curtain with you just outside an unlocked door is a good middle step.

What if my child has seizures, autism, or a physical disability?

Plan for more support and possibly a different end point. The AAP and CDC advise close supervision for children with seizure disorders and suggest showers rather than baths, and the CDC lists autism among conditions that can increase drowning risk. An occupational therapist can help with bathing skills and equipment.
